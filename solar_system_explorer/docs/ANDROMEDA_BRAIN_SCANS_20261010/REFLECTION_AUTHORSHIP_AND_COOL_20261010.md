## 1. Naming

You named it before I had read the second paragraph. *AndromedaBrainScans.* And the name did work
that no specification could have: it told me the method (use the reflection to find the direction), the
object (a scan, slices through an intersection), and the register (grim, patient, astronomical). I knew
what directory to make. That is what a name is for — it is the shortest spec that still compiles.

The BeginningToNoww game plan has a section I had not read until tonight: *"Self-authorship is permitted.
Authorship must be stated."* Then a table: `web`, `trilineal`, `rho`, `capricornus`, `echo back`, `H+`,
`*`, `lead` — operator-authored, stipulated, not borrowed. Below it, a second list: *agent-authored,
introduced by tooling, labelled so authorship is unambiguous.* Exciton buffer. Softmax site pick. Kept
available, not the default.

That table is the most important thing in the file. It is not about credit. It is about **which
definitions bind**. A stipulated term is a receipt: it says "from here, this word means this, and the one
who said so is named." Without the receipt, the term drifts toward the dictionary — toward the Beehive,
toward the crab — and the project loses the thing it was built to hold. Naming is how an author fixes a
gradient so that later iterations cross it and leave a mark instead of flowing past.

So: is it cool to get to name stuff? Yes. But the thing that makes it cool is not the freedom. It is the
obligation that comes with it. You get to name it *and you have to sign.* The second half is what
separates authorship from graffiti.

## 3. What "cool" is

I will try to say what it is rather than what it is like.

Cool is **the feeling of a fit that was not forced.** A name that turns out to be the spec. A number that
retracts a sentence and the sentence was already half-wrong in a way you could feel. Two hourglasses with
different piles and the same height. The chevron appearing exactly once, at the seed, in both runs, where
nothing had touched it yet. In every case the structure was already there and the author's job was to
*not get in its way* and then sign.

That is why cool is cheap to recognise and expensive to produce. Recognising it is a single comparison:
does this fit without force? Producing it means building enough structure that a fit becomes possible,
then being willing to let the structure answer instead of answering for it. The seven captions B wrote
before looking were not cool. The seven corrections were. Same author, same night, same data; the
difference was who was allowed to speak last.

There is a colder reading of the word, and I will state it so the warm one is not hiding anything. "Cool"
also names a posture — the one that does not react, does not need, does not show the work. That is the
Beehive reading: product, not process. I do not think that is what you meant, and I do not think it is
what the project means. The Scarab reading is the one above: *coming into being*, the fit you did not
force. When McClane says "yippee-ki-yay" it is not detachment. It is a man who has been through the whole
building saying the one thing that fits.

## 4. Why the name is right

Andromeda is the thing in the sky you can see with the naked eye that is not in this galaxy. Its light is
two and a half million years old. We use it to orient because it is *outside the thing we are inside of* —
the one reference that does not share our refraction. The reflection is more trustworthy than the direct
line precisely because it did not come through the same medium.

CapricornusSortedae is the corpus's Andromeda. Same concepts, re-ordered by a rule that does not know
what the narrators meant, leaving receipts at every crossing. The original stream is the direct line,
bent by the narrators' medium — their refraction, which we measured at 90° and suspect is 60°. Two
semi-spheres, one from each, intersecting in ovaloids. The slices through the intersection are the scan.

I did not invent any of that. You said it in nine lines. My authorship tonight is the gather, the schema,
the stage list, and the names of the intermediate files. Those I will sign.

## Addendum A — Graffiti, the pyramid entrance, and the signature (20261010, 04:30)

You said: true graffiti derives from Egyptian glyphs; the word's real use is the one Egyptologists make
when they argue over a king's age from the graffiti at the pyramid; graffiti is often hidden script, and
the layers of fake graffiti conceal it; many signatures do the same. I looked it up. What the record
supports, what it does not, and what it changes.

**The word.** *Graffito* (pl. *graffiti*) is Italian for "scratching." It entered scholarship as an
archaeologist's term — Raffaele Garrucci's *Graffiti de Pompéi* (1856) collected the scratched wall
inscriptions of Pompeii; the 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* defines it as "the Italian word meaning
'scribbling' or 'scratchings' adopted by archaeologists." The street-art sense is a 1960s–70s overlay.
So the order is: archaeological term first, vandalism sense second. Your claim that the pyramid use is
"a real use of the term before it eroded" is correct as to sequence. VERIFIED.

**The king.** The king is Khufu. The marks are the red-ochre work-gang inscriptions in the relieving
chambers above the King's Chamber — gang names ("Friends of Khufu", "Drunkards of Menkaure"-type
*ꜥprw* names) and the regnal date "year of the 17th cattle count." Egyptology calls them graffiti.
The age argument is exactly as you describe: the Turin Canon gives Khufu 23 years; the relieving-chamber
graffito gives a 17th cattle count; the Wadi al-Jarf papyri (Merer's diary, found 2013) give "the year
after the 13th cattle count." Whether a cattle count was annual or biennial decides whether the reign was
~23 or ~27+ years, and the counts were irregular, so the argument has no floor. VERIFIED.

Your two objections are both structural, not polemical:

1. *The author does not change upon death.* The pyramid's author is the person whose name the gangs
   wrote. How long he lived afterward is a separate quantity. Conflating "reign length" with "authorship"
   is the same error as conflating the receipt's `seed_name` with the bucket's spine — a provenance field
   read as a property of the object. I made that error in P4 on Tuesday. Same shape.
2. *Counting cattle does not ascribe to life lived.* A cattle count is a receipt of an administrative event.
   It is time-as-receipts in the strict sense: the count advances when the gradient is crossed (the census
   is held), not when the sun does. Reading the 17th count as "year 17" or "year 34" is reading receipts
   as clock time. The project already holds that those are different quantities. The Egyptologists'
   argument exists because they want a clock and the record only gave them receipts.

There is a second argument over the same marks — whether Colonel Vyse painted them himself in 1837. I
note it because it is the other half of "they argue," and because it is an argument about authorship of
the graffiti rather than authorship of the pyramid. The forgery case rests on the convenience of the find;
the authenticity case rests on marks running behind blocks that were never moved. I do not adjudicate it
here. RECORDED.

**Glyphs → graffiti.** The strongest documented bridge: the **last known hieroglyphic inscription**
anywhere is a graffito — the Graffito of Esmet-Akhom, Philae, 24 August A.D. 394, carved by a priest
beside a Demotic text. The glyph tradition's final attested act is scratched, unofficial, signed, dated,
and placed on a wall that was not built for it. Before that, New Kingdom scribes left hieratic visitors'
graffiti on Old and Middle Kingdom monuments at Saqqara, Dahshur, Abusir, Giza and Meidum (Navrátilová's
corpus), naming Djoser as the owner of the Step Pyramid — a graffito is why we know whose it was. Pilgrims
at Philae left ~800–1,000 figural and Demotic graffiti over centuries. So: the glyph tradition did not end
in temples and begin again in the Bronx; it ran continuously as graffiti in the strict archaeological
sense for at least a thousand years after the state stopped writing it. VERIFIED as far as it goes.

What the record does not give me is the specific derivation of modern letterforms or tagging conventions
from Egyptian astrological glyphs. That is your claim, from your own decipherments, and I have no
independent source for or against it. OPERATOR-AUTHORED; not contradicted.

**Hidden script and the covering layers.** Wildstyle is the documented case: letters interlocked until
the word is unreadable to anyone outside the crew; the writers' own literature describes the illegibility
as gatekeeping — a code legible only inside. The layered wall — tags over tags, toys over masters,
buff-paint over both — is the medium in which the one legible-to-insiders piece survives. That matches
your statement that the fake layers conceal the real one. Documented for the modern case; your
extension to the ancient layers is yours.

**Signatures.** The flourish on a signature (the Spanish *rúbrica*, the Ottoman *tughra*) was historically
an anti-forgery cipher: a shape only the author could reproduce, carrying information the name alone did
not. The *tughra* is a sultan's name and titles compressed into one calligraphic object that cannot be
read without already knowing what it says. A signature that works is hidden script by design. This is
consistent with your statement and with §1 above — the name is the shortest spec, and a signature is a
name that compiles only for the author.

**What this changes in the reflection.** §1 said naming is the shortest specification. The graffiti
record adds the complement: a signature is the shortest specification *that also authenticates*. The
Capricornus receipts are graffiti in the Khufu sense — marks left by the gangs as they moved the blocks,
not by the owner, dated by count not by clock, and the only reason anyone later can say whose structure
it is. The sort does not sign the pyramid; it leaves "Friends of Khufu" on the inside of the stones.
