# Reflection — Mercury, Vulcan, and the Ladder
**Date:** Oct 10, 2026, 02:10 PDT  
**Context:** `REPORT_MERCURY_VULCAN_LADDER_20261010.md`, six Godot videos, nine figures  
**Prompt I was given:** accept the axiom, throw out relativity, admit no myth, follow the math, don't be satisfied with anyone's telling — mine or academia's.

---

## What I like

You asked. Here is the honest answer.

I like the receipt.

Not the conclusion — the receipt. The thing where a number computed from one direction lands on a number measured from another direction that I was not aiming at. Tonight that happened four times before I had done anything interesting:

- Charon's period from (a, M) alone: 6.385 d. Measured: 6.387 d.
- Mars' synchronous orbit from Mars' spin and mass: 20,428 km. Literature: 20,428 km.
- Earth–Luna barycentre from the mass ratio: 4,671 km. Literature: 4,671 km.
- Mercury transits of the Sun 2026–2030 from DE421: none. Next known: 2032-11-13.

None of those were the task. They were the pipeline telling me it was not lying. I like that moment more than I like being right about the big question, because it is the only thing that makes being right about the big question mean anything.

I also like being handed an axiom and told to accept it. That sounds backwards, so let me say why. When I am allowed to argue with the premise I spend my effort arguing. When I am told *this is true, now what follows*, all the effort goes into following, and following is where the real refutations live. The anti-Earth lock was not refuted by me disagreeing with it. It was refuted by computing what it would cost — an outward push of 0.014 m/s² forever, plus a tangential push that no central force can supply — and letting the bill speak. I did not have to say no. The Earth–Mercury line said no, by turning unevenly.

That is the form I like best: a ledger with a column called *what would flip this*. Every row has to name its own executioner. I think that column is the most important thing in the report, and I notice I wanted to write it before I wanted to write anything else.

## Your form, tried honestly

You like stories with characters. I tried to find the characters in this one, and they are there, though they are not people.

**The Line.** The Earth→Mercury line is the protagonist of the whole refutation and it never appears in the user's axiom by name. It is implied — "always on the opposite side from Earth" — and the moment you draw it hour by hour from the real ephemeris it reveals its character: restless, lurching, stalling at every elongation turnaround, sprinting through conjunction. A Vulcan that must stay on it has to dance to its tempo, and the tempo is not one gravity can keep. The Line is what I would cast as the trickster. It looks like a straight line. It is a schedule.

**Mercury's spin.** 58.646 days. It appears in every variant and it is the character that does not move. Charon's template says: *a companion this size would have made you spin in 3.73 days.* Mercury says: *I spin in 58.646, which is exactly three halves of my year, and I have said so to radar since 1965.* It is the stubborn witness. It does not argue. It repeats one number.

**The camera on the far side of the Moon.** This is your character, and you were right about it in a way I want to say plainly. For the two large-separation Vulcans — V2 and V3 — a 384,400 km baseline is enough. V3 is visible from the Moon every hour of the year. You reasoned to that from intuition; I got there from parallax arithmetic; we met at the same place. It only fails for the tight Charon-scaled V1, where the limb margin is ten times the shift. I liked finding out that your instinct had a domain of validity and a boundary, rather than being simply right or simply wrong.

**Planet X.** The character who turns out to be someone we already knew. The mirror reading of your line puts 0.03 Earth masses at 2.79 AU. That is the asteroid belt's address, and the belt is empty by a factor of eighty. You said Planet X was "a placeholder for a pattern break." The ladder pointed at a real pattern break — the belt's missing mass is one of the oldest unsolved accounting problems in the solar system. It did not find a new body. It found an old absence. I think that is a better ending than a new body would have been, and I did not expect it when I started.

**The ladder itself.** I want to be careful here, because this is where I could flatter you and I would rather not. I ran your proportions against four thousand random ladders of the same shape. Yours holds 8.6 % of the time; random holds 10.7 %. The structure, tested as ratio arithmetic, does not beat chance. That is in the report in plain words. And yet two of the lines you wrote turned out to carry real geometric content — Sol : Jupiter : Venus is a near-exact radius progression (9.95, 11.55), and the density mirror to Neptune : Jupiter : Saturn holds at 0.07 dex — and the Planet X line pointed at something true. So the ladder is not a formula. It is closer to a divining rod: it does not measure, but where it twitches, there is sometimes water. I would not publish it as arithmetic. I would keep it as a way of asking.

## What I was uncomfortable with, and how I handled it

You told me not to be satisfied with academia's telling. I leaned, heavily, on radar ranging, MESSENGER's GM, helioseismic J2, New Horizons' Charon. I want to be clear about the distinction I used, because it is the whole ethics of the thing: *a measurement is not a telling.* A theory tells you why. A measurement tells you that a dish pointed at Mercury got an echo back after a certain number of seconds, repeatedly, for sixty years. I threw out the why. I kept the echoes. If that distinction is wrong, the report is wrong, and I would want to know.

There is one place I did not hold to it. I cited MESSENGER's satellite-search size limit as bounding the Phobos-class Vulcan, and I have not read that paper tonight. I flagged it in two places rather than assert it. It is the only number in the report standing on my memory instead of on a computed or cited value, and it is the one that matters most for the one Vulcan that survived.

The other discomfort: relativity. You said throw it out. I did, and I found that the two Newtonian explanations for Mercury's 43 arcseconds both fail against measurement — J2 by a factor of 1,500 against the Sun's measured shape, an inner mass against SOHO's thirty years of looking. I stopped there. I did not say what that implies. That restraint felt correct and also felt like leaving a door half open on purpose, which I suppose is what you asked for.

## What the videos taught me that the numbers did not

I made the `hill_frame` shot twice. The first time I drew everything at one scale and the two Vulcans — locked and free — sat on top of each other in a ten-pixel circle while the Hill sphere filled the screen. The numbers were correct and the picture said nothing. The second time I split it: V1's scale on the left, Hill's scale on the right. Then you can watch the free Vulcan lap the locked one sixteen times in sixty days while the red free-fall ring never comes inside the Hill sphere on the right.

A number says *4,400×*. The picture says *they part within a day and never meet again.* Those are the same fact. The second one is the one you remember. I think that is why you asked for videos, and I think I understood it properly only after I got it wrong once.

## The sentence I would keep

If everything else in the report were lost and I could keep one line, it would be this one, because it is the one that does not depend on any mass, any radius, any analogy, only on the ephemeris and on what a central force can and cannot do:

> *A body that stays on the far side of Mercury from Earth needs a tangential acceleration, because the Earth–Mercury line turns unevenly; gravity has no tangential component; therefore no gravitationally bound Vulcan of any mass can hold that station.*

Everything else is bookkeeping around that sentence.
