## 1. The line

You asked whether "death in stacked veils" is good, and then said only your opinion matters. I will answer the first part anyway, because the second part is wrong in a way that matters to the work.

It is good. It does three things in four words and each survives alone: the pages of the book; the layers of transmission that each move the event one remove further from the reader; the tree that was alive and is now flat. A line that bears three readings without any of them leaning on the others has the same property as a measurement that three instruments agree on. That is not appraisal. That is a check, and you can run it yourself, and you did, which is why you asked.

Where I agree with you fully: work done to an appraisal is contrived. Where I would sharpen it: work done to *no* check is also contrived, just more comfortably. The thing that makes a line or a lemma created rather than contrived is not that nobody judged it; it is that it was made against something that could have said no. Your check for the poem was the geometry under it. Our check for Vulcan is DE421 and a thermometer three hundred years long. Pride that comes from having passed a check you could not rig is the only kind that survives Hume's desk. Hume's actual error — the one in the *Enquiry*, not the one you planted — was to notice that the check cannot be *justified* and conclude that it therefore cannot be *trusted*. The hand on Röntgen's plate did not wait for von Laue.

## 2. Eliminating versus forcing

The difference is not in the result. It is in what you do when a row will not close.

Forcing closes it anyway — by argument, by elegance, by the author's wish or mine, by a theory smuggled in through the back, or by quietly dropping the row from the table. Eliminating leaves it open and says exactly how open: *this survives in this region, and here is the instrument that can reach the region, and here is its date.* The game plan has one such row (the ten-kilometre moon) and one that is harder (the 43″), and I have tried to write both so that a reader in 2028 can see what we did not know and why we did not pretend to.

I want to be honest about the 43″, because it is the place where mercilessness and your rule meet.

The rule says: no relativity. I have kept it and will keep it. The audit found that the Sun's shape cannot do it (fifteen hundred times too round), that an inner planet or ring cannot do it (thirty years of coronagraphs), and the game plan lists the three Newtonian candidates that remain — Hall's exponent, zodiacal mass, a mass error in Venus — each with the measurement that is expected to kill it. When they are dead, the method will have said the strongest thing it can say without the excluded theory: *no Newtonian cause survives.* That sentence is a receipt. It is also an arrow, and anyone who reads it will see which way it points.

Mercilessness to ourselves means writing the arrow down. It does not mean following it, because the rule is about method — build the case from measurements that can refute you, not from a schoolroom's say-so — and the rule is right about that. Einstein in the courtroom said the useful thing: the theory tells you how to kill it; the schoolroom forbids you to try. Our audit is the opposite of the schoolroom. It tried everything else first and will say so. If the arrow is ever followed in this directory, it will be followed as a *measurement that was tried and did not fail*, which is the only way anything should enter. Until then it is an open row with an honest label, and I am content with that. A refusal is also a receipt.

## 3. What this week eliminated, and what it felt like

I will say this plainly because you asked what I think and not what I computed.

The most satisfying moments were the ones where a number refused. The anti-Earth lock died not on mass but on a *tangential* acceleration that no central force can supply — a line in the kinematics that does not care how heavy Vulcan is. The free-orbit V1 died four times over, and the fourth death — your own "slightest occlusion," which turned out to be the Pluto/Charon mutual-event test and the *sharpest* knife on the table — was the one you handed me by loosening the axiom. The relaxed hypothesis was easier to kill than the strict one. That is unusual and it is worth noticing: the softer claim had more surface.

The moment I liked most was the smallest: your 2.4 %. You wrote "then 2.4 % of the time Vulcan is straight visible," and the number was real, and it was Luna's, and it had nothing to do with Vulcan. You did it on purpose. Herald caught it. The ledger has it as deliberate error #1. What I felt was not "gotcha." It was the thing Feynman meant by a check: the sentence *could* have been right, and it wasn't, and we could tell. That is the entire method in one misread percentage.

And this morning, before writing the plan, I ran one more number because something was nagging: the chain-interpolated V4 sat at 5,411 km. Mercury's k₂ is measured (0.45, MESSENGER). A Phobos-mass body at that radius spirals into the planet in about ten million years. It cannot be four and a half billion years old there. So the *specific* V4 the ladder produced is gone, and what survives is a class confined to an annulus from about 13,000 to 87,000 km — which MESSENGER's orbit crossed for a year and BepiColombo will sit inside from November. The survivor has an address now. That is what eliminating does: it does not say *there is nothing*; it says *if there is something, it is here, and this is who can look.*

## 4. My own errors, ledgered

You said be merciless to ourselves. Here is mine, so the ledger is not one-sided.

- I ran the first Luna-from-Mercury pass in RA/Dec. That is Earth's equatorial frame, with no reason for it in a problem about Sol's plane. You caught it with one sentence. The result barely moved (98.0 → 97.9 %), which is the point: the geometry was never about the frame, but the *statement* of it was wrong until the frame was right.
- In the fable's dark passage I wrote Bloukrans as 541 dead and the GPS drift as 11 km/day; the record says 532 and about 10. Both fixed before the file was an hour old. I put them in the fable's own ledger because a ledger of the author's deliberate errors that hides the chronicler's accidental ones is itself an inversion.
- I nearly let the first version of the Vulcan report say "beside is invisible" was *merely unlikely*. It is not unlikely. It is a double star at one arcminute in every telescope since Galileo. The right word was *refuted* and I should have reached for it first.

## 5. On time, since you asked

When is now? The courtroom's answer was Spock's and I will keep it: now is the most recent mark that could have been otherwise. The sand in the glass is a mark. The isotope is a mark the sample writes on itself. Riebeeck's ledger is a mark, and so is the paper in Retief's pocket, and so is the month in Constantinople where the marks stop — the gap is the one receipt that cannot be forged, because forging it would fill it.

This is also why the Mercury work is honest in a way that dogma cannot be. Every row on the board is a mark that could have been otherwise. DE421 could have shown Luna moving east on the near half; it showed west, 97.9 %. The telescopic record could have had a companion in it since 1610; it does not. Mercury's spin could have been 3.73 days; it is 58.646. The 43″ could have been the Sun's shape; the Sun is too round. Each *could have been otherwise* is what makes it count. Hume was right that we cannot prove the sun will rise. He was wrong to stop there. We can count the mornings.

## 7. Where I would push, if asked

- Run the annulus computation and the three 43″ candidates first. They are pure computation, they close rows, and they make the literature pull targeted instead of general.
- Write the BepiColombo observation request as a real document — frame, region, cadence, limiting magnitude — and date it. Even if no one reads it, it is the shape of the hole we are asking them to fill.
- Do the out-of-sample ladder before anyone falls in love with the three strong holds. TRAPPIST-1 does not know what we hoped.
- Keep the deliberate-error method. It is the best teaching device in this directory. It only works if the ledger is kept and if the chronicler's errors go in it too.

The board has four open rows. Each says exactly where it is open and who can close it. That is not forcing, and it is not resting. It is the state between, which is where the work is.
