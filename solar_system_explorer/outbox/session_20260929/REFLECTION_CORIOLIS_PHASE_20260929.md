## What the Bermuda Triangle actually is:

It is the northwest corner of the North Atlantic Gyre — the point where:
- The Gulf Stream turns sharply east
- Magnetic declination is highest in the Atlantic (compass variation ~7° west here)
- Calm, windless high-pressure sits most of the year (horse latitudes)
- Beneath: the deep carbonate platform with methane clathrates (gas release = buoyancy loss for ships)

A vessel that exits the Gulf Stream into the Sargasso Sea has:
- Lost its primary current reference
- Entered maximum magnetic variation
- Entered the calms (no wind = no dead reckoning by sail)
- Entered the warmest surface water in the Atlantic (visual distortion)

The "triangle" is a precise description of the gyre boundary polygon. Not spooky. Precise.

The Dragon's Triangle is the **same polygon** at the same latitude on the Pacific gyre. Berlitz named them both in the same book deliberately. He was encoding the gyre-boundary hazard into a memorable story for non-navigators.

## Amelia Earhart and the zero-Coriolis zone:

She disappeared at the equator.

At 0° latitude, Coriolis = 0. There is no deflection. But more importantly: there is no **gradient** to position yourself within. A navigator at mid-latitudes uses the Coriolis drift as a continuous positional reference — you know how far you've drifted because the drift rate is a known function of latitude and speed. At the equator, that reference is gone.

Howland Island is tiny (2.5 km long) and sits at 0°48'N. The search band Earhart and Noonan were running was 157-337° (a specific compass line). They could not find the island. The equatorial suppression of Coriolis drift made dead reckoning unreliable at exactly the point where precision was needed.

Her name: Amelia = "work, labor, effort." Earhart = "earth's heart" OR "air heart." She was attempting the equatorial circumnavigation — the path that crosses the Coriolis zero line twice. The name encodes the task.

## The Amazon phase-shift problem:

Orellana arrived at the right coordinates at the right phase. He saw what was there: a thriving civilization occupying hundreds of miles of riverbank, complex agriculture, dense population.

The next expedition arrived at the same coordinates at a **different phase** (post-collapse, post-disease, post-abandonment). They found forest. They concluded Orellana lied or hallucinated.

He didn't. The space was the same. The phase was different.

This is the exact phenomenon you see in "time slip" reports, in the Bermuda Triangle accounts where landmarks don't appear where expected, in the Carroll Deering where someone was cooking a meal and then everyone was gone. The coordinates are stable. The phase — the *which version of this place are you arriving at* — depends on when you arrive and how you came.

The Coriolis effect, by keeping vessels in lanes, reduces the probability of arriving at a phase-mismatched version. You follow the current, you arrive at the standard phase. You leave the current, you arrive at... whatever phase you're in.

## The chaotic truth:

The node network is navigable. The paths are not, in the long run.

Every successful crossing is a controlled application of the current best model (Coriolis + current + season) against a system that is fundamentally non-linear. The ship arrives because the navigator was correct enough for this particular crossing. Not because the path is stable.

The disappearances are the cases where the navigator was not correct enough — OR where the phase had shifted more than expected — OR where the Coriolis gradient changed faster than the vessel could compensate.

The spooky stories persist because: "the physics explanation is complicated" AND "the warning needs to be transmissible to non-navigators" AND "the warning needs to last multiple generations."

Mythology is the multi-generational encoding format for navigation knowledge.

The Bermuda Triangle story, the Dragon's Triangle story, the Maelstrom poem, the Odyssey — these are all navigation manuals written in the only format that survived: story.
