Two categories:

**Outbound travelers** — artificial objects launched from within the helioregion, now leaving or departed.

### 2.2 1I/ʻOumuamua — The First Interstellar Visitor

**Discovered:** October 19, 2017 (precovery images from October 14 and 17)  
**Designation:** First interstellar object detected. Named ʻOumuamua = "scout/messenger from far away" in Hawaiian.

**Orbital data:**
- Eccentricity: 1.20 (hyperbolic; threshold = 1.0)
- Inclination: 122.7° — retrograde relative to planets, steeply inclined
- Perihelion: 0.256 AU (inside Mercury's orbit — Sol passed it at very close range)
- v∞: 26.33 km/s (speed relative to Sol before entering field)
- Incoming radiant: Ecliptic longitude 284.4°, ecliptic latitude **+56.8°** — 56.8° above the ecliptic plane
- Direction: 6° from the solar apex (the direction Sol moves through the galaxy). Statistically expected — most interstellar objects would come from this direction.
- Origin constellation: Lyra (near Vega) — but this is geometric, not physical; it was not "from Vega"

**Path through the system:**
- Entered from north of ecliptic, high angle
- Passed below the ecliptic on September 6, 2017
- Perihelion September 9, 2017 at 0.256 AU — inside Mercury's orbit
- Closest approach to Arya: 0.162 AU (October 14, 2017) — after perihelion, receding
- Departed toward Pegasus

**The anomaly:** Non-gravitational acceleration — the object accelerated outbound beyond what gravity alone predicts. This was confirmed in multiple papers. Proposed explanations: radiation pressure (requires extreme flatness), outgassing (no coma detected), light sail (speculative). The anomaly remains open. The object was not re-observed after leaving due to fading magnitude.

**Incoming from above:** ʻOumuamua came at ecliptic latitude +56.8° — this is the steepest approach angle of the three known interstellar visitors. It entered from high above the ecliptic plane and bent southward through perihelion. "Almost straight down" is a reasonable characterization at 57° to the ecliptic.

### 2.3 2I/Borisov — The First Interstellar Comet

**Discovered:** August 29, 2019 by Gennadiy Borisov (MARGO Observatory, Crimea)  
**Designation:** 2I/Borisov. First confirmed interstellar comet.

**Orbital data:**
- Eccentricity: 3.36 (strongly hyperbolic)
- Inclination: 44.05° — moderately inclined, not retrograde
- Perihelion: 2.006 AU (near inner edge of asteroid belt — between Mars and intrasolar barrier)
- v∞: 32 km/s
- Incoming radiant: RA 2h 12m, Dec +59.4° — constellation Cassiopeia, near Perseus border
- Direction: ~75° away from the solar apex — NOT from the apex direction, unlike ʻOumuamua. Came from the galactic plane direction, not the halo.
- Departure direction: Telescopium (southern constellation)

**Cometary activity:** Clear coma and tail observed. CO, H₂O detected. Behaved like a "normal" comet, just from outside the system. Nucleus size ≤ 0.5 km. Composition essentially identical to solar system comets — confirming interstellar comets carry similar chemistry.

**Travel time:** In interstellar space, takes ~9,000 years to travel one light-year relative to Sol.

**Key contrast with ʻOumuamua:** Active comet (coma confirmed). Nearly ordinary. ʻOumuamua was dry, elongated (10:1 aspect ratio estimated), anomalously accelerating. Borisov was chemically familiar. They are very different objects from very different source environments.

### 2.4 3I/ATLAS — The Most Extreme Visitor

**Discovered:** July 1, 2025 by the ATLAS survey (Chile facility, code W68). Initial designation A11pl3Z.  
**Full designation:** C/2025 N1 (ATLAS), later 3I/ATLAS.

**Orbital data:**
- **Eccentricity: 6.14** — by far the most hyperbolic trajectory ever recorded in the solar system. ʻOumuamua was 1.20. Borisov was 3.36. 3I is 6.14.
- **Inclination: 175.1°** — 4.9° from perfectly retrograde. Moving in the opposite direction to all planets. 
- Perihelion: 1.357 AU (between Arya's orbit and Mars) — perihelion October 29, 2025
- **v∞: 58 km/s** — fastest interstellar object detected. Arya's orbital velocity is ~30 km/s. 3I arrives at twice Arya's speed.
- Discovery position: RA 18h 07m, Dec −18° 41′ — in Sagittarius, near the galactic center direction
- Discovery distance: 4.50 AU from Sol, 3.50 AU from Arya

**The retrograde geometry:** Inclination 175.1° means 3I came in nearly anti-parallel to the planets' orbital motion. It did not come "from above" like ʻOumuamua. It came in the plane of the solar system but going BACKWARDS. Its relative velocity to solar-orbiting bodies is Sol-velocity + 3I-velocity = ~58 km/s + 30 km/s = up to ~88 km/s encounter velocity for objects in prograde orbits. This is the highest encounter velocity of any known interstellar object.

**Active cometary chemistry (JWST detection):** H₂O, CO₂, and CO volatiles confirmed. This is the first direct measurement of interstellar cometary composition from a known extrasolar visitor. 3I was held at interstellar temperatures (≲10 K) for potentially billions of years before entering the helioregion — the volatiles survived.

**Current position (as of Feb 26, 2026 — last reported observation):**
- RA: 7h 0.8m, Dec: +20° 4′ — constellation Gemini (near M44's neighborhood)
- Distance from Sol: 4.600 AU (beyond Mars, approaching asteroid belt distance but outbound)
- Distance from Arya: 3.977 AU
- Speed relative to Sol: 61.2 km/s (slower than v∞ = 58 km/s because now climbing out of Sol's gravity well)
- Last observed magnitude: 15.9

**Close approaches made:**
- Mars: October 3, 2025 at 0.194 AU
- Arya minimum approach: 1.798 AU (December 19, 2025) — inside the asteroid belt. Never close.
- Venus: November 3, 2025 at 0.65 AU
- Jupiter: March 16, 2026 at 0.358 AU — passed Jupiter close, now heading outward

**Departure:** Heading outbound through Gemini/Auriga. Will reach the inner Oort cloud (~2000 AU) by approximately 2189. Will reach the outer Oort cloud edge (~100,000 AU) in ~8,000 years.

**The retrograde observation:** 3I came in the ecliptic plane going the wrong way. From a Sol-centered viewpoint: all planets move counterclockwise (prograde). 3I moved clockwise (retrograde). It passed Mars, nearly passed Arya's orbit, passed Venus and Jupiter — all at extreme encounter velocities. It was never going to be caught by the planets and it was never going to be captured by Sol. At e = 6.14, the Sun's gravity barely bent its path.

## Part V: On Transmission Through the Helioregion

The user raises a precise question: electromagnetic transmission from beyond the heliopause operates in "its least effective medium."

The observation is directionally correct. Within the helioregion, the solar wind plasma creates a charged-particle medium. Radio waves propagate well through this plasma (at the frequencies used: 2.3 GHz and 8.4 GHz for deep space communications). However:

- The plasma frequency of the interstellar medium is very low (~few kHz). Radio waves well above this propagate without issue.
- What IS reduced outside the helioregion: the signal strength due to pure distance (inverse square law). At 170 AU, Voyager 1's 23-watt transmitter is received by the 70-meter DSN dishes at a power of roughly 10⁻²⁰ watts — 20 billion billion times weaker than the transmit power. This is not a medium problem; it is a distance problem.
- What the helioregion boundary DOES affect: the magnetic field structure. The interstellar medium has a different magnetic topology than the heliosphere. Charged particles (not photons) behave very differently across the heliopause. The Voyagers directly measured this transition in 2012 and 2018 — abrupt change in plasma density, particle energy, magnetic field orientation.

The signal Voyager 2 is currently sending exists simultaneously in the heliosheath AND the interstellar medium (it crossed in 2018). The transmission works. The field structure that surrounds it has changed. What the solar field "knows" about Voyager 2 (via solar wind particle interactions) is now very limited — Voyager 2 is outside the field. The electromagnetic signal is the only remaining channel.
