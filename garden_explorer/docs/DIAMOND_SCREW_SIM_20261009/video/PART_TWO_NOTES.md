## Who the hooks resolve to

**1. Aristarchus of Samos (c. 310–230 B.C.)** — the heliocentric model. His own
treatise on it is lost; the surviving attestation is **Archimedes**, in *The Sand
Reckoner*, addressed to King Gelon of Syracuse: "Aristarchus of Samos brought out
a book consisting of certain hypotheses… that the fixed stars and the sun remain
unmoved, that the earth revolves about the sun in the circumference of a circle."
So the "peer reviewer" is Archimedes, not Socrates (Socrates died 399 B.C., ~90
years before Aristarchus). This is a gift for the video: Archimedes is already
seated in the circle from Part One, holding the glass. He can simply stand up and
say he wrote it down.

Archimedes also records Aristarchus' answer to the obvious objection — if the
earth moves, the stars should shift (parallax) — namely that the sphere of fixed
stars is so vast that the earth's orbit is as a point to it. That is the thread
that runs straight to item 3.

**2. Plutarch (c. A.D. 46–119)** — Greek under Rome, wrote the *Life of Antony*
(the primary literary source for Cleopatra: the barge at Tarsus, the pearl, the
asp). Separately, in *On the Face in the Moon's Orb*, Plutarch writes that
Cleanthes thought Aristarchus "ought to be indicted for impiety for putting in
motion the Hearth of the Universe" — i.e. Plutarch is the one who records that
heliocentrism got Aristarchus accused of impiety. Same author, both facts. So
"the Roman who recorded whatever about Cleopatra" is Plutarch, and he carries the
Aristarchus story forward. (Strictly he was a Greek citizen of Rome; "Roman" in
the loose sense.)

**3. Giordano Bruno (1548–1600)** — burned at the stake in Campo de' Fiori,
Rome, 17 February 1600. Nuance to preserve: the charges were theological
(Trinity, transubstantiation, Mary, plurality of worlds, etc.); Copernicanism and
the infinite universe were in the mix but he was not tried *for* parallax
specifically. The honest framing for the video: Bruno took Aristarchus' answer to
the parallax objection — the stars are so far that you see no shift — and pushed
it to its conclusion: the stars are suns, the universe is infinite, there is no
"sphere" at all. He mentioned the thing that follows from parallax being
unobservable, and was burned. Stellar parallax itself was not measured until
Bessel, 1838 (61 Cygni). So from Aristarchus to Bessel is ~2,100 years of "no
parallax" — exactly Feynman's "things you could be wrong about, and weren't."

**4. Franklin / the elephant.** Two things to separate:

- *Benjamin Franklin* did propose electrocution as a humane slaughter method
  (letters, 1750s — he electrocuted turkeys with Leyden jars and knocked himself
  flat doing it: "I meant to kill a turkey, and instead I nearly killed a
  goose"). He is in the circle from Part One with the jar.
- *Topsy the elephant* was electrocuted at Luna Park, Coney Island, on 4 January
  1903. She had killed a man (a drunk handler who burned her trunk with a lit
  cigar — "the mean rider" is a fair description). She was killed with
  **6,600 volts AC** supplied by the local utility, plus poisoned carrots and a
  rope; Edison Manufacturing Co. filmed it (*Electrocuting an Elephant*). It is
  often misremembered as an Edison "war of the currents" stunt against AC; the
  war of the currents was effectively over by 1893, and it was AC, not DC, that
  killed her. **She was not burnt to dust** — the carcass was dismembered and
  disposed of; there was no incineration.

So if Franklin "bursts" into Part Two with this story, Widukind's ledger should
quietly correct him: *AC, 1903, Coney Island; the cigar was the rider's; not
dust.* That is the Widukind move from Part One (Kant's "exactly as instructed"
reduction) and keeps the video educational rather than mythic. Feynman would
score the correction; McClane would score the fact that the grid Tesla left on in
Part One is the thing that did it.
