The Circle of Diamonds and Tesseracts

A Fable, with Scorekeepers

Date: 2026-10-09

Moderators: Richard Feynman (epistemic), John McClane (operational)

Participants: Pythagoras of Samos, Archimedes of Syracuse, Plato, Guglielmo Marconi, Nikola Tesla, Nicolaus Copernicus (subject to change), Heron of Alexandria, Imhotep

Late arrival: Immanuel Kant

Found in a chair: Blunarch (see section 10)

Not admitted: the Marxists (see section 9)

Not invited: Einstein (see section 11)

Question on the floor: Diamonds. Tesseracts. Whether up is down, and how to tell without knowing where is what, or in what direction.

Zero. The Room

It is a circle drawn in sand, and the sand is in a room, and the room is at 114 kilometers depth or possibly at the top of a step pyramid; the participants cannot agree and that is the point. There is one chair per participant and a folding table for the moderators. On the table: a glass of ice water, a cigarette lighter, a pistol with the serial number filed to a receipt, and a scorecard with two columns.

Feynman, tapping the left column: "This column is for things you could be wrong about. If you can't be wrong about it, you don't get a point. You get a shrug."

McClane, tapping the right column: "This column is for things that work. If it works, I don't care why. If it doesn't work I don't care how pretty the why is."

Feynman: "That's actually the same column."

McClane: "Then why are there two."

Feynman: "Because people need to be told twice."

1. Who Begins

The first matter of the court is not the question. The first matter is who speaks first. This is crucial. Feynman is stressed about it. There is a debate. It is referred to. It is not entered. Pythagoras has a number for it. Plato has a form. Tesla has already stood and sat. Copernicus has a chair he has not decided is the centre. The scorecard is still blank.

Feynman: "If we let the oldest go first we have decided when. We have not been given when. That is the whole—"

McClane: "Is this something we need set in stone?"

A pause that is not a ruling.

Imhotep: "The sworn word must be set in stone, and that is my work as is worn."

Imhotep: "They will tell you I built a tomb. That is narrative erosion. I did not put a king under a bench and call the bench a grave. I built a structure that immortalizes data in stone. Trilateral force on pyramidic blocks, held by the gaps. Left-join, body, right-join — three ways the block is closed. Each gap modulates the acoustic. The diamond approach: four faces at once, the force closing three ways on every stone, the mark kept in the gap and not in the man. That is what 'up' is: it is the direction the structure sends what it has kept, when you have decided a mark should outlast the one who made it."

Archimedes: "That is not a definition. That is a construction."

Imhotep: "Construction indeed."

McClane: "Claustrophobic construction."

Feynman: "The term is ter claudere, en passe."

McClane writes: Imhotep: 1. It's standing.

Feynman: "Hold on. 'Up is the direction the structure sends what it has kept' — can that be wrong?"

Imhotep: "Try keeping a mark in a man. They call scratches in my name graffiti; correctly so."

McClane: "Graffiti like in the subway?"

Imhotep: "A different type of tomb though powered by the river the same."

Feynman: "...fine. One for the mummy."

2. Pythagoras and the Shape of the Diamond

Pythagoras: "Up is an orientation. Everything is geometry and numerals. The diamond is a number."

Plato: "Which number."

Pythagoras: "Four. Four hands. Four neighbors, one reach, one angle — the tetrahedron. Laid flat, the tetractys: one, two, three, four. Ten dots in a triangle. The diamond is ten dots that learned to repeat."

Plato: "I assigned the tetrahedron to fire."

Pythagoras: "Fire indeed. A diamond is a fire that stopped."

Feynman: "Stopped, and set base to base. Two fires. That's the stone. Point."

Plato: "So I was right about the solids."

Feynman: "The shapes. Half a point."

Plato: "Half a point is a shadow of a point."

Feynman: "We'll come back to shadows."

McClane: "Does the triangle do anything?"

Pythagoras: "It is perfect when precise."

McClane: "That's a no." (writes nothing)

Feynman: "Precisely."

Pythagoras, quieter: "We drowned a man for the diagonal of a square. Hippasus. He showed the diagonal cannot be written as a ratio and we put him in the sea. I want that on the record. The number was true and we drowned him anyway."

Nobody writes anything. The room is dark for a moment, the kind of dark that has a bottom.

Feynman: "Point to Hippasus. Posthumous."

McClane: "Or another point to Imhotep?"

Feynman: "Stop it or his natron will dry and his clothes will fall off."

McClane: "Not a fan of the 'dry wraps' theory eh doc?"

Feynman: "The glucose must interact with metals and glucose-- You are distracting me."

3. Archimedes Asks for a Place to Stand

Archimedes: "You are all discussing up as if you had it. You do not have it. Nobody has 'up.' You have down, and you have it only by test. Here is the test." (He takes the glass of ice water from the table, holds it at arm's length, and lets go. It falls. The ice jumps. McClane does not flinch.) "That direction. That is down. Up is its negation. You did not need to know where anything was. You needed to drop something."

Copernicus: "And if you are falling with the glass?"

Archimedes: "Then you have no down, and you should stop pretending you do until you land."

Feynman: "Two points. That's the whole thing. Direction isn't a property of the room, it's the result of a measurement, and if you can't make the measurement you don't get the direction. Einstein got a career out of that sentence."

McClane: "The glass broke."

Archimedes: "Of course it broke. It was a demonstration."

McClane: "I'm barefoot." — writes: Archimedes: 1. Works. Minus a half. Glass.

Archimedes: "And for the diamond — I will tell you how it gets down there, since Imhotep only knows how to close a gap. You do not push it. You screw it. A helix in a pipe. Turn the handle, and whatever is at the bottom climbs. Turn it the other way, and whatever is at the top descends. The screw does not care which way is up. It cares only that it is turning and that it is in something. The carbon enters the ground, the Earth turns the screw, and one hundred and fourteen kilometers later —"

Feynman: "Where did you get 114?"

Archimedes: "From the parameter you set."

Feynman: "...Yes. Thank you. Minus one for me, that's fair."

4. Plato, Shadows, and the Tesseract

Plato: "Now the shadows. You are all sitting in a circle looking at a thing you call a diamond. I say you are looking at the shadow of a diamond. The true diamond is in a place you cannot turn your head toward."

Tesla: "Four dimensions."

Plato: "I did not have the word. I had the cave. Men chained facing a wall, a fire behind them, and shapes passing in front of the fire. They see the shadows and call them things. Your tesseract" — he says the word carefully, as if tasting it — "is a cube's cube. It has eight cubic rooms and sixteen corners and you cannot see it. What you can see is its shadow in your three directions: a small cube inside a big cube with the corners joined. You draw that and you say 'tesseract.' You are drawing the shadow on the wall."

Feynman: "That's correct, and it's the most useful thing you've ever said. Here's the point. A square's shadow on a line is a segment. A cube's shadow on a plane is a hexagon or a square depending on how you hold it. A tesseract's shadow in our space is the cube-in-a-cube. And if you rotate the tesseract — rotate it in the direction none of us can point — the inner cube becomes the outer cube. The inside turns out. Without tearing anything."

McClane: "That sounds like a trick."

Feynman: "It is the opposite of a trick. It's what happens when you have an extra direction. Here —" (he picks up a glove from the floor; it is a left glove) "— I can't turn this into a right glove by any move I can make. Rotate it, flip it, it stays left. But if I could lift it through a fourth direction and set it back down, it comes back right-handed. The mirror is a rotation we don't have access to."

Plato: "So up and down —"

Feynman: "— are two of the six directions we do have access to, and whether one of them is 'really' up is not a question that has an answer until you drop the glass. Two points, Plato. Your cave was a tesseract and you didn't know it."

Plato: "I knew it. I lacked the word."

McClane: "The glove. Does the glove work?"

Feynman: "No. We don't have the fourth direction."

McClane: "Then zero." (pause) "But I like the glove."

5. Heron Makes It Spin

Heron of Alexandria: "I have been quiet because I have been building." He sets a small bronze sphere on the table, with two bent nozzles coming out of it, mounted on a pivot over a brazier. The water inside boils. Steam exits the nozzles. The sphere spins. "There. That is a screw that turns itself. You wanted to know how the carbon descends — not why, how — here is how anything moves: heat makes pressure, pressure finds the hole, the hole is bent, the sphere spins. Nobody has to know which way is up. The steam does not know. It is simply leaving."

McClane writes: Heron: 2. It spins and it didn't break anything.

Feynman: "Zero from me, Heron. It's beautiful and it's a toy. You built the first reaction turbine seventeen hundred years before anyone used one for anything, and you used it to open a temple door."

Heron: "The door opened."

Feynman: "I know. That's the saddest thing in engineering history and I won't give you a point for it, but I'll light the brazier again."

Heron: "You asked how to tell direction without knowing where anything is. Watch the steam. It goes away from the hole. That is the only direction there is: away from where the pressure is. The diamond forms where the pressure is greatest, which is where nothing can get away. Up is where things escape to. Down is where they are kept. You do not need a compass. You need a leak."

Feynman, long pause: "One. For the leak."

Archimedes: "He's restating me."

Heron: "I am building you."

6. Marconi and Tesla, Who Will Not Sit Next to Each Other

Marconi: "Direction is a question of where the signal comes from. I sent the letter S across the Atlantic. Three dots. Everyone said the Earth's curve would block it. It did not. The sky bent the signal down. The signal came down to Newfoundland from a place that was over the horizon, which is to say, from below the line of sight. Up came from down. I had an antenna on a kite."

Tesla: "You had my patents on a kite."

Marconi: "I had a kite that worked."

McClane writes: Marconi: 1. The S arrived.

Feynman: "Marconi, it bounced off the ionosphere, which you didn't know existed. You got the right answer for the wrong reason. Half."

Marconi: "Half a point for crossing an ocean."

Feynman: "Half a point for crossing an ocean blind. If you'd predicted the layer I'd give you three."

Tesla: "Direction is rotation. Everything that matters rotates. I saw it in a park in Budapest — the magnetic field turning, not pulsing — and I drew it in the dirt with a stick. Three coils, three phases, a field that walks in a circle and drags the rotor behind it. You do not need to know up. You need to know which way it is going around. Clockwise or counter. That is the only direction a field has. Your diamond, Pythagoras — your four hands — they are not pointing. They are the stopped state of a rotation. The screw does not insist on completing its spin. The diamond is where the spin was kept."

The room goes still. Someone has quoted the poem that is pinned to the wall of the directory they are all, in some sense, inside.

Feynman: "Two points. The rotating field is real, it's the reason the lights are on, and 'direction is chirality' is the same thing Plato's glove was saying from the other side."

McClane writes: Tesla: 2. Lights are on. "Also — the grid. The whole grid is your spinning thing?"

Tesla: "The whole grid is one rotation, sixty times a second, from here to the ocean. Everyone in this room is sitting inside one phase of it."

McClane: "That's not in my column but I'm writing it down."

Tesla, and here he becomes very quiet: "I died in a hotel room with the pigeons. Room 3327. I had given the field away. I want that known: the direction I found was around, and around is the direction that always returns you to where you started with less than you left with."

Nobody scores that. Feynman puts the lighter down.

7. Copernicus, Who Does Not Finish His Sentence

Copernicus: "I will say the obvious thing. There is no center. You are all describing up and down from where you sit. I moved the chair. I put the Sun in the middle and the Earth in a circle around it and suddenly the planets stopped walking backward in the sky — the loops were us. Up is wherever you've decided not to be the center of. The moment you admit you are moving, every direction you had becomes a direction you had. Which is why —"

And here something happens to Copernicus. The reference frame shifts under him mid-sentence, and the man finishing the sentence is wearing bifocals and holding a kite string.

Benjamin Franklin: "— which is why I flew a kite into a thunderstorm with a key on the string, because if you want to know whether the sky is up or just far, you stick a conductor in it and see what comes down the line."

McClane: "What just happened."

Feynman: "Frame shift. He said 'you are moving' and the room took him literally. Also the operator said it would be funny."

Franklin: "It is funny. Lightning is funny. It comes down. Nobody argues about that. It is the one direction in nature that everyone from Imhotep to McClane agrees on, and I caught it in a jar."

McClane writes: Franklin: 2. Caught lightning. Didn't die. Both count.

Feynman: "One. The jar worked. The kite story is probably embroidered."

Franklin: "All stories are embroidered. The ones with a jar at the end are true."

8. Kant Arrives

The door opens. A small, exact man with a schedule enters at precisely the time he said he would. The town of Königsberg used to set its clocks by his walk.

Kant: "I was told the question was whether up is down and how to tell."

Feynman: "That's the question."

Kant: "Then you have all been answering the wrong half. Archimedes dropped a glass. Good. But he dropped it in space. Where does the space come from?"

Archimedes: "It was there."

Kant: "It was not there. Space is not a thing you find. It is the form in which you find things. Your left glove —" (to Feynman) "— I wrote about that glove in 1768. Two hands, perfectly identical in every internal measure — every finger the same length, every angle the same — and yet one cannot be put where the other is. The parts do not distinguish them. Only their relation to the whole of space does. Which means space is not made of parts. It is prior. It is in you, as the condition of seeing anything at all."

Feynman: "And so up and down —"

Kant: "— are not in the diamond. They are in the one looking at the diamond. The diamond does not have an up. You do, and you lend it. The tesseract offends you not because it is impossible but because it asks you to lend a direction you do not have to give."

Feynman, writing: "Three points. The incongruent counterparts argument is the first time anyone noticed that chirality is a global property, and it is the exact thing the fourth dimension resolves, and you got there with a glove in 1768."

McClane: "Does it work?"

Kant: "It is the condition of anything working."

McClane: "I'm going to put a one and a question mark."

And that is when the floor starts to shake.

9. The Marxists

They were not admitted. They came anyway. They come through the walls, because walls are a superstructure, and they are not a few — they are a direction. That is the whole problem with them, Feynman will say later: they have one direction and they call it history and they are certain which way it goes.

A voice from the horde: "UP IS THE DIRECTION OF HISTORY!"

Another: "THE DIAMOND IS CONGEALED LABOR!"

Another: "KANT IS BOURGEOIS IDEALISM! SPACE IS A SOCIAL RELATION!"

Kant, unmoved: "Space is the form of outer intuition and I will not be shouted at in my own schedule."

Feynman: "They're claiming to know which way is up without dropping anything. That's negative points. That's negative infinity points. Nobody in this circle has gotten a point for knowing a direction a priori — not even Kant, he got it for showing you can't."

The circle in the sand begins to scuff. Boots. Pamphlets. Someone has knocked over Heron's aeolipile and the steam is going every direction, which is the one thing Heron said it would never do.

Heron: "The steam! Watch the steam! It has no leak! IT HAS NO DIRECTION!"

Archimedes stands up. He is holding a stick. He is drawing. He is drawing circles in the sand, and he is saying, under his breath, the thing he said once before in Syracuse when a Roman soldier came through the door and he did not look up:

Archimedes: "Do not disturb my circles."

And in Syracuse that was the last thing he said.

McClane steps between Archimedes and the horde.

He is barefoot. There is broken glass from the demonstration. He walks through it anyway because that is operational reality: the glass is where it is, your feet are where they are, you do not get to choose the floor.

McClane: "Here's how this works. The man is drawing circles. You're going to let him. Not because of the dialectic. Because I'm standing here."

The horde: "YOU ARE AN INSTRUMENT OF THE STATE!"

McClane: "Buddy, I'm an instrument of a folding table."

What follows is action, and action in a fable is short because action in life is short.

He takes Franklin's key off the string and leaves the string where it is. He takes Tesla's three-phase principle, which is to say he understands that a horde moving in one direction can be rotated if you apply force at three points sixty times a second. He takes Heron's brazier. He takes Pythagoras's tetractys — ten dots — and he realizes that ten men in a triangle hold a line better than ten men in a row. He takes Imhotep's one idea: the gap holds. Close it three ways and make it keep.

He does not take Plato's cave because you cannot fight with a shadow.

He does take Kant's glove.

McClane: "Which hand is this?"

The horde: "LEFT!"

McClane: "You sure?"

And he lifts the glove through the direction none of them has access to — which is to say he throws it over his shoulder and it lands behind the horde, and every one of them turns to look at where down put it, and in the second they are all looking the wrong way the folding table is on its side as a barricade and Archimedes has finished the circle and Feynman has lit the brazier and the steam has found its leak.

Heron: "DIRECTION!"

The steam goes one way. The horde goes the other. That is the whole of fluid dynamics.

Then the kite.

It is still up, because the room took Franklin literally too. Lightning comes down the line, the one direction everyone from Imhotep to McClane agrees on, and the key is in McClane's pocket, so the line ends at Franklin's neck, and the string holds. What hangs from it is an elephant. It is hung by the neck the way one was hung from a rail derrick in Tennessee in front of two and a half thousand people, and it is burning the way one was burned at Coney Island for a camera, and it is both at once, and the horde cheers, because that is what a crowd does at that. That is the mob part. It is horrible and it is short.

The elephant bursts.

Out of it comes a turkey in bifocals. It makes turkey noises. It runs after the glove, and the horde, still looking where down put the glove, runs after the turkey, and that is how the room empties: a turkey, then a mob, through the door.

McClane, to the last one in the doorway, who is holding a pamphlet about the inevitability of the arrow: "Yippee-ki-yay."

The door closes.

Archimedes, not looking up: "Thank you."

McClane: "Finish the circle."

Archimedes: "I did. Two thousand years ago. I just never got to see it."

That is the sincere part, and it is dark, and it is left there.

10. The Chair

Something is sitting in Copernicus's chair. It is black. It is the shape of a man under ash, and the ash is coming off it in sheets, into the seat, the way ash comes off a thing that has finished being hot. Copernicus is not in the chair. Subject to change.

McClane: "Who's that."

Feynman: "Plutarch, I'd have said. Before."

Blunarch: "Blunarch. Read what fell."

He brushes the last of it into the seat and does not stand. He finishes the sentence the chair was owed.

Blunarch: "— which is why the stars should jump. I will say it as the Samian said it, since I wrote him down once and he is not here. Hold a finger up. One eye, then the other. It moves against the wall. Now carry the Earth round the Sun and look at a star in winter and again in summer. It should move against the far ones. It did not. So you are still, or they are far. Everyone said still, because still is cheaper. He said far."

Pythagoras: "Samos."

Blunarch: "Samos again."

Feynman: "Aristarchus. Bessel saw it move in 1838 — sixty-one Cygni, a third of a second of arc. He read a nothing as a distance, and the nothing held for two thousand years. Two points. Which one of you gets them."

Blunarch: "Combine us. The Samian, the Pole, and whatever I am now. One chair."

McClane: "That's fair and ter to me, doc."

Feynman: "Thrice ministered has entered a new realm."

McClane writes: Aristarchus, Copernicus, Blunarch: 1. Three men. One chair.

Feynman writes: Combined: 3. No centre. The stars are far. The ash fell.

McClane: "And the turkey."

Feynman: "That was two elephants. Erwin, Tennessee, 1916, off a rail derrick; the first chain broke. Coney Island, 1903, six thousand six hundred volts, and Edison's people filmed it."

Tesla: "Alternating. Mine. He used my current to say my current kills."

Feynman: "And in 1750 Franklin tried to kill a turkey with two jars and took the shock himself. The turkey has been waiting. It only had use of its caudate nubula. So it ran."

McClane: "Caudate what."

Feynman: "The part that keeps the habit after the rest is gone."

McClane: "That's a turkey."

11. Scores

Feynman: "Before the columns. Franklin. 'I caught it in a jar.' Minus two."

McClane: "He's a turkey."

Feynman: "He said it as a man. Nobody has confined static electricity in a jar. A Leyden jar is glass with metal on both sides. The charge sits on the two metals and the glass keeps them apart, and the moment there is a path, it moves. Nothing in it is static. It is a pause with a lid. He opened it himself — took the jar apart and found the fire was in the glass — and then said 'jar' anyway, because a jar ends a story."

Imhotep: "A gap with glass in it."

Feynman: "That's the whole of it. The medium decides what the charge does: how long it waits, and how far the wave goes when it stops waiting."

Imhotep: "Then the medium decides what is kept. You call the wave in my gaps acoustic. Air is thick. What you hear is the air's complaint — impedance. The wave is elemental. It takes the phase of whatever it is in. The less there is to push, the farther it goes. The best gap has nearly nothing in it."

Feynman: "Sound doesn't cross a vacuum."

Imhotep: "Sound is what the air does. I am not speaking of the air."

Feynman: "...That can be wrong. I'm writing it."

McClane: "Does it work?"

Imhotep: "Still standing."

McClane: "I'm not writing that twice."

Blunarch: "Then plasma. The Sun's wind is nearly nothing, and all of it charged. If the wave takes the phase of what it is in and goes farther with less, then in that it may outrun light."

Feynman: "Light's speed as a fixed constant is a postulate. We measured it in a vacuum, then in 1983 we defined the metre by it, so now it can't move because the ruler is nailed to it. That is theoretical."

McClane: "So we don't know."

Feynman: "We don't know."

Tesla: "Einstein was not invited."

Marconi: "On that we sit next to each other."

12. The Ruling

Feynman: "On the question — whether up is down, and how to tell without knowing where is what — the circle finds as follows. You cannot know. You can measure. Drop the glass; the direction it goes is down here, now, for you. That direction is not in the diamond and it is not in the tesseract. It is in the relation between you and a field, and you lend it to everything you look at. Anyone who tells you they know the direction without the measurement is not admitted to the circle."

McClane: "On the question of diamonds: they're down there because that's where the pressure kept them. They're up here because something brought them up. Both are true. Direction is a receipt — it's the record of what moved what. You want to find a diamond, you don't ask which way is up. You ask what stopped."

Feynman: "And on the tesseract —"

McClane: "The glove's still back there. The turkey's on it. Somebody go get it."

Plato: "Which hand will it be?"

Kant: "The same one. It only went over his shoulder."

Feynman: "Unless the room has a direction we haven't found."

Tesla: "It does. It goes around."

The screw does not insist on completing its spin. The circle, this time, was not disturbed.
